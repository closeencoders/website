My AI is Bigger Than Yours

Will we lose a generation of would-be experts to nihilism because they believe robots are good enough for most tasks?

By Josh - Apr 18, 2026 | posts | ai, rant, sarcasm

Until recently, people were not as afraid to share valuable ideas. The pervasiveness of social media and blogging shows that humans want to create, share, and connect.

Anything of value released into the wild, with or without proper protections such as licensing backed by enough money to sue, is part of a never ending game of cat and mouse. The difference now is that this information is extracted at scale with obscene efficiency by web scrapers, bots, and patent trolls searching for complex rent-seeking opportunities to avoid falling behind others doing the same.

What does value creation really mean anymore? Is it merely unexploited data used to further an agenda or produce alpha? Regardless of AI, has it always been that way, only now reaching a scale that bothers people?

It's as if AI companies were created by people that believe whoever is left standing with the biggest and baddest AI model will find the ‘One Piece’ and become king of the AI startups. They forgot to kill some guy on a big podium though. I don’t remember the details, sue me.

I am not sure why anyone would train their AI using my code, or similar code, but I’m sure it has happened. I envision a newbie prompting ChatGippity with a niche question, only to receive a solution similar to mine, with an O(n!) algorithm and a dataset large enough to keep it looping until the eventual heat death of the universe. Yet still parade around as if the discovery is groundbreaking.

I don't want to sabotage anyone, nor do I really think that happens, but as I am learning, the first draft of my code is often junk. Much of the publicly available code is junk too, and most people don't claim perfection or publish everything for a reason.

No matter how much data is collected, we are limited by what information that data reveals once processed. Only when a system seeks data for itself and toward it's own ends autonomously can it be truly intelligent. Something I see the average person doing less of every day, not because they can't, but because they don't want to.

I will avoid the obvious Star Trek reference to the Custodian and Q, and the Incredibles' "everyone's super" cliché. Instead, I want to talk about nihilism, or at least a subtle form of it which shines doubt on the future. Belief is powerful, whether it's true or not doesn't change that fact. Ideas can still determine a person’s behavior which can lead to undesirable results.

All jokes aside, I’m sure our bad practice projects are filtered out of AI training data. Right?

Illusion of Creation

It’s easier than ever to copy what comes out of various generative AI products and spam it across the web. Unless you have sufficient prompts and context to mask it, the output is obvious, giving its signature robot vibes from the “average” of every shared opinion, research paper, and project the model was trained on. The output feels soulless because it lacks applied thinking and reflection by the user which the system just can't fully imitate, even when asked to.

Many professions now require you to compete with those who fully embrace AI at scale. Driving this, a subtle but ever-present gravitational pull toward the path of least resistance for what is perceived as the most output possible. With so much desire for a product that makes thinking “automated,” AI products may continue to improve far into the future, assuming they find a way to turn a profit that is.

The path of least resistance is not a suggestion but a law of nature, and while AI is not objectively bad, the way some people use it isn’t great, to say the least. Even reasoning models themselves seem to follow this principle, as researchers scramble for performance cheat codes and scalability.

Access to information and automation are some of the main pros of modern society, and I’m sure you already know the obvious arguments against AI. The world has already proven that ethical, moral, and environmental concerns will not stop people from doing what they want, unfortunately. It’s the irony in having advanced tools, but being too senseless to wield them safely, which seems to be a trademark of human nature.

What I am worried about instead is the death of experts. Not the generic gatekeeping and annoying jerk behind a phony degree or nepo-executive position “expert.” I’m talking about people who truly love their craft, hyper-fixate on learning one thing, solve complex problems, and have the desire to pass this knowledge forward. The type of mentor I wish I had but never did.

Stuff that may never be found in an AI training dataset, or even be excluded, because it’s a deep experience that matters only from the perspective of an original thinker who does not post garbage on X, Reddit, and LinkedIn all day. Also, who gets to decide what is “quality” or “ethical” data? If I use wording a web scraper or AI doesn’t agree with, even if I’m right, will that mean it's not worthy?

Don’t get me wrong, smart people will find ways to use AI as a tool and enhance whatever it is they are doing and still learn the material because they don’t really have a choice, for the time being. However, I am afraid that for many in the coming years, they may also lose the desire to “get good” at a craft they otherwise would have, even just as a hobby.

Illusion of Ownership

The generic “Oh no! People will have no brain now” argument is a recurring theme. The same thing was said about search engines and how Google is a monopoly on access to said information. The fear was the similar, people would “Google” the information they needed and never “think” again. For example, in the software world, it was a common complaint that junior developers would copy and paste code from Stack Overflow without understanding what it does or how it works and learn nothing from it.

I can assure you these Stack Overflow posts were not sourced in the junior's annual review.

Does Thinking Create Ownership?

In the case of search engines you are often forced to click on a link that takes you to a website where the alleged creator of the content could, at least in theory, attempt to get credit for what they did. Alternatively, if you have the resources, go through a publishing process to help ensure your name is stamped on said property or that its patented before you release it into the wild.

This feeble attempt at ownership drives much of (fortunately not all) the incentive to create and share value. It’s far from perfect, piracy and plagiarism dilute the idea of owning “digital media,” but it works well enough that people still make software, write books, create content, and provide services online all the time.

This prompts the question: Does feeding information through a complex system and then regurgitating it through generative AI avoid the need to source external material? So far, the argument has not been completely settled, as attribution, copyright, and ownership aren't really the same thing legally.

Several cases against OpenAI, Microsoft, and various other institutions have been filed or are still pending. For example, the notable New York Times case in 2023 and the GitHub Copilot case, along with a few others left many feeling uneasy about the interpretation of copyright laws going forward. As long as you can convince a court that a system does not violate the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), then it’s fine, at least for now.

In many cases, a license will permit free use of property within certain limits, but for many licenses, you still must cite the original creator of that property. Even if an individual would hypothetically never receive money for the property, simply having their name attached to it is the value. Particularly in open source, people use that attribution on their resumes to aid in finding a job or as prestige, which in turn often does translate to monetary value.

One argument can be found in the paper titled Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models by Nicholas Carlini which was dismissed by the J. DOE 1 vs. GITHUB, INC case. This paper outlines that models have been observed to "memorize" training data nearly verbatim under the correct circumstances. If taken at face value, this would pose an issue to the claim that a models don't violate at least some portion of DMCA.

In a subsequent blog post, one in which I found absolutely spectacular in its level of detail titled Provable Copyright Protection for Generative Models which eludes to a paper to back it up. The author of the post at one point argues:

"In a new paper, we (Nikhil, Sham, and Boaz) provide a formalism that enables rigorous guarantees on the similarity (and, more importantly, guarantees on the lack of similarity)  between the output of a generative model and any potentially copyrighted data in its training set."

As AI Improves, Will Sourcing Also Improve?

Another counter argument I see is that AI should simply output all the sources for given data after every prompt, becoming a sort of search engine replacement of sorts. This sounds great, but in reality, when it does provide sources, even when asked, the links and citations are often poor quality, consisting of opinionated Reddit posts, content not fully related to the prompt, or links that are simply broken and lead nowhere.

Particularly in academic settings, the distrust surrounding AI training and the threat of hallucinations have led many to believe their work has been absorbed by an unknowable blob. While there is significant room for debate, the argument has enough merit that many organizations are stretching the narrative to aid in their own agenda, ill intentioned or not.

As the technologies surrounding AI improve, the sourcing problem may improve with them. It has not escaped me that better prompts, a deeper understanding of reasoning models, and specialized agents can already improve the quality of an AI’s output. Even so, these developments leave me with more questions than answers.

*I am still reflecting on these questions and considering how best to integrate them into the post, if at all.

Low Hanging Fruit

  • Who ultimately decides what sources are used to train models and why?
    • Will there be a priority system for sources, such as an example where the Z source matters more than the Y source because of X, and who gets to decide X? Is this determined by a poll, another AI using a guided metric, or something else?
  • Which of the possibly millions of sources used will be shown to the user when they ask for it, and why?
  • Similar to search engine results, how biased can LLM output be in favor of a specific narrative that the model creators want?
  • How do you know when an AI is lying when the question is complex? This assumes that you know more than the AI or have easy access to alternative information that has been validated. This is similar to Googling with the intention of finding the result you want and then confirming your bias.

Higher Hanging Fruit

  • Can you patent what comes out of an LLM without using sources? What happens if major countries just decide one day you can't, what happens to all the vibe coded projects?
    • Can you 100% prove or disprove a project used AI? Who gets to decide the final verdict on whether AI was used or not?
    • Will the "amount" of AI used decide if a project is worthy or not for copyright and licensing? Will it be a spectrum or some subjective metric like tokens used to lines of code or something?
  • If we need more regulations on how AI is used or trained for some unknown reason far in the future, who is going to stop people from using it?
    • "Ask AI" is becoming synonymous with “Google it” for some, and I assume AI will be deeply embedded in critical infrastructure like it or not.
    • I suspect that “guardrails” on AI will not stop those who understand how to leverage it from doing whatever they want if they have access to the right hardware.

Illusion of Power

Imagine using an LLM to make a new product or piece of content, do you really want to see a list of all the sources and all related patents for every prompt?

The power to generate something fast comes with responsibility. Seeing the sources would mean your history of interacting with the LLM could hold you accountable for plagiarism or patent infringement, even if unintentional.

And yes, unless you run it locally or it explicitly says otherwise, everything you prompt an LLM with is potentially saved long-term. It is not held to the same security standard as other systems are, like a bank or medical data for example, at least not yet. So if you freely give up your medical info, PII, or evidence for a crime, that’s on you.

Most who say otherwise, including the ones letting you use their robot, are lying to you.

Imagine you do something illegal, or someone falsely accuses you of doing something illegal and you go to court, the court may try to query for it, and it will become public information if ruled. This has already happened, and I doubt it will be the last time.

Even if not publicly available, it's clear someone will have access to the data and know exactly who prompted it. It’s too much like giving a chainsaw to a child for a government to ignore. People just willingly enter their deepest and darkest thoughts they would never share otherwise, as if it were their therapist.

When something is free or cheap, to the point that the world’s largest companies are willingly spending billions of dollars without an obvious way to make a return yet, you have to question why. It’s just how the world works now. Anyone in the space with a brain knows that the last ones standing with the best data also have the most power.

They will not be held accountable in the same way you or I would be when they are caught doing something wrong to get that data. Like stated before, especially if the dang thing is so deeply integrated into society and infrastructure that no one could afford to do anything about it anyways.

The idea that you can ask whatever you want from a free or cheap LLM “secretly” and you can do whatever you want will always be an illusion. The same goes for anything on the internet. You don't own the AI or the data center, some corporation does. If you really want this “power” for yourself, you have to build the infrastructure yourself and run it locally.

Companies selling you a subscription to use their pet robot for a monthly fee don't want the credit to go anywhere else, because then their pet robot is subject to copyright laws. Just like any business, investors don’t want heavy competition, or for their product to be subjected to complexity, they want a super deep moat.

Alpaca Giraffe Iguana

I’m not entirely against AI, see the image above for this post. I generated a “funny” image to make a point because it’s obvious I didn’t make it, then added some bait text on it in GIMP to really emphasize the low effort slop look.

Most AI systems, especially LLMs, are just overhyped, power hungry tools attached to fancy web scrapers running in a data center. They have value, but I don’t really care.

What I am not a huge fan of is the motivations of the people who train and host these tools. They present them as replacements for things that make no sense, use scare tactics to manipulate people, and steal data because it boosts their “stonks” for shareholders in the hope of making more money.

As an investor myself, it’s insulting.

The only thing that saddens me more than misaligned incentives and manipulation is hearing people say, “You don’t even understand how AI works, bro,” whenever someone makes an argument to the contrary. As someone with real world experience using it, I can assure you that most people who claim to understand, in fact, don't. That does not mean they are “idiots.” People can still understand the gist. Those incentivized by returns, rather than by improving humanity as they claim, may not actually care what happens to you while using their product.

That does not just apply to AI.

Who Has The Power?

In a previous life, we used AI for tasks it should not have been, or we would be punished as a team. It was used to write garbage code, the review process was virtually nonexistent, and the only thing that mattered was velocity so management could lock in a bonus. The job didn’t start out that way, I hoped it would go back to normal after a while, but it continued getting worse.

Yet I still forced the code to work, I was one of the people at the company to fully adopt it into my workflow despite pushing back originally. They even used some of my ideas and never gave me credit for it. Even then, that would have been fine as long as the job hadn’t turned into a sleepless 80+ hours a week nightmare.

Getting past that, people were starting to not understand how the system was working at a fundamental level and disregard security measures for the sake of speed. I understand this can happen in larger code bases as complexity grows, but I mean the system itself was running in a way we would eventually have no meaningful control over, but still be expected to force it to work.

This was in the best interest of the Product Owners at the time. If the developers and QA had no meaningful say or power over what was or wasn’t working, even when it clearly wasn’t, the feature could be forced to production. From there, it was easy to have people like me and the offshore team (if you know, you know) fix it over the weekend or address it as a “patch” instead of a “bug fix” in the next sprint, without spending more time on paper.

This way, we could still claim that AI and poor decisions were making the process faster. It’s easy to blame the developers for not being “skilled enough” with AI when, in reality, the product breaks because you agreed to short deadlines without asking what it would really take to build it properly.

With or without AI, it still has to go through QA and UA, often requiring additional tweaks to satisfy end users. If I had some control over whatever systems we were adopting and handled the configuration, I could have at least tried, but due to bureaucracy, I was not allowed to try.

I had no real power, so no matter what I did, it was irrelevant.

When we brought this concern to someone I trusted, the only person who had both the leverage to push back and the willingness to do so, they told me they planned to leave once that became the norm. Notice that they did not say if. This was a hint that I should do the same.

And for the record, I cannot get behind “quiet quitting.” I deeply cared about the quality of what we were doing while no one else did, so I quit. Despite past manipulative attempts to keep me there, one of which went as far as threatening to ruin my career.

If AI, and everything else in general, were viewed objectively as tools that may or may not aid in specific situations, instead of through what feels like mass hysteria, situations like this would not happen. AI would never have developed such a stigma. This has become an issue for millions of people, not just those working in software.

I hope it's clear that AI did not cause issues, people did.

Who Cares?

It’s not just LLMs. There are other applications for AI that are not often talked about in the news, and nobody knows what the real limitations and consequences will be. So much so, they can’t get people like Alex Karp to shut up about how “great” it is.

Oddly enough, I actually like Alex's enthusiasm, I would kill to be enthusiastic about AI, but I'm just not.

To wrap this up, I doubt superintelligence is on the radar anytime soon, if ever. I also doubt AI will be a mass drag-and-drop replacement for people in the way it is advertised in the near future either. But maybe it doesn’t need to be, if it's “good enough,” do you think the model itself, the company that created it, or a government wielding it will care about you, your prompts, your SaaS project, your job, or anything at all really?

If anyone could click a few buttons to deploy a billion dollar business, why would any rational organization give that away? At the point of “super tool,” why would anyone stop at exploiting profit? If that happens, people have a lot more to worry about.

That’s the appeal of the Necronomicon, and our current generation’s iteration of it. In past instances, it was the the splitting of atoms or the internet perhaps. Those who gaze upon it's pages begin to believe that humanity is insignificant, pointing to cosmic horrors as proof that hope is futile. It is a parasitic form of nihilism, a belief that digs into the mind and promises something greater will whisper its secrets of knowledge and power, but this is only a work of fiction.

Will people even care until it directly impacts them several years from now? I’m not sure I truly care deep down anymore either.
