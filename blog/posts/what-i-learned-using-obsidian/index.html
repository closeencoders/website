Building a Second Brain in Obsidian: Lessons from 4 Years of Note-Taking

After four years of building a second brain in Obsidian, I share what worked, what failed, and why perfectionist note-taking holds you back.

By Josh - Sep 10, 2026 | posts | learning, note-taking, productivity

I despise the idea of applications trapping my data, and often try to build my own dumbed-down versions for a higher degree of freedom. I'm a bit of a pirate in that sense, and that’s partially why I’m writing this. To help take control of your notes, not the other way around!

I was hooked by the idea of a second brain after watching videos by Ali Abdaal and reading the book Building a Second Brain. I’m not affiliated with either, but if you love taking notes, they could be worth a look.

Significant time has passed as I’ve tried to make the idea work, and my opinions have changed. Obsidian feels freeing with its heavy customization and plugins, but it’s a trap in disguise for hoarding information and overthinking your notes. Except for the specific plugins mentioned I don’t want these ideas to be limited to Obsidian, but to come from my perspective as someone who has used it.

I condense most issues I’ve encountered over the years into two core ideas and present my case for working through them.

  • Creating notes that are easy to find and reference.
  • Maintenance hell from overworking format, plugins, and quality.

Creating Notes

Stopping the note-taking process from getting in the way of your work is key to making the notes work for you.

The most important thing you can do is make the note accessible to your future self without spending too much time away from your work. Everything else is irrelevant if you can’t find the information or remember that it’s there. This may sound obvious, but this is the number one reason people say “I take lots of notes but never use them.” If it's frustrating to find the notes, then why would anyone ever use them?

Once this is mastered, everything else, like plugins and wiki style linking becomes a bonus, not fluff. Otherwise, it's probably better to handwrite notes or use simple documents like Google Docs.

Structure

Fleshing out a unique title, filename, and topic summary with solid keywords before anything is the most effective use of time. If the keywords expressing the topic of your note are overly generic, too specific, or spelled wrong, you won't find them. The first iteration of a note does not have to start out that way, it can evolve as you process the information onto the page.

Don’t focus on making the content completely coherent either, only to convey enough you wouldn’t otherwise remember with correct spelling. Like a complicated set of steps, a list of references, or a quote with an author’s name you want to use in a paper or book later. Don’t let it venture into hoarding material for later, keep it on the topic at hand and let it be chicken scratch.

Moving into an example, if I make a very generic note about cows, it will only convey the meaning to my future self that the file is about cows. The note's contents become too abstracted away from the user and force you to search through it.

I made heavily simplified visuals in Markdown, but I think it gets the point across.

Cow.md

# Cow

Their stomachs are divded into 4 compartments. They digest grass slowly in stages.
If you farm cows, it's important to note this process.
(https://external-cow-source.zom)
I found this interesting because XYZ...
What do you call a cow with now feet? Ground Beef!
...

This file can only be found with a filename search of “cow,” which forces me to do a mass search for specific information about the cow with something similar to (ctrl + f) or (ctrl + shift + f). I deem this type of note a “God Note” where the contents eventually don't contribute any real meaning. In this case, it becomes easier to Google “cow” for more reliable and up-to-date information.

This does not mean having a Cow.md or a Farm.md with generic information is bad. The point is only that broad, higher-level notes provide less context, so I use them only when they have a clear purpose.

Solid cases for broad files are dashboards, hubs, and categories, especially when the entire body of notes is on a specific subject. In this case, it may make sense to use Cow.md (or maybe something like Agriculture.md) as a kind of index page that links to everything and the key ideas, but all the subtopics on cows could still remain in their own note files to keep them isolated, searchable, and reusable. Most note-taking applications like Obsidian have built-in functionality as well as several plugins for dashboards.

Unlike dashboards, I would argue that the process of collecting hundreds or even thousands of tidbits of information to support a topic and then compiling all the information into a single file with lots of headings is more like writing an essay. This is completely fine if that’s your goal, but there’s a way to make notes easier to find.

Cow Stomach.md

# Cow Stomach

Cows stomachs are divided into 4 compartments have which aid in the digestion of grass more slowly in several stages.
I found this interesting because XYZ...

This file is specifically about “cow stomachs” and a process associated with it. If I was learning about the process of nutrient extraction in digestion and searched “stomach” or “cow,” these notes show up. Now this note about cow stomachs can be linked to anything for its isolated subject rather than to everything I know about cows.

This means that notes will not be interconnected based on the "God Note" without context, and should be easier to find. Take this next note about cow farming pulling in cow information as an example of what I mean.

Cow Farming.md <-- Cow Stomach.md

# Cow Farming

It's important to know how [[cow stomach]]s process what you feed them so you can keep them healthy.
I found this interesting because XYZ...

With this, Cow Stomachs.md is now linked to Cow Farming.md without attaching everything you've ever learned about cows in Cow.md without context. This simultaneously avoids duplicating the source on cow digestion as you continue adding notes to related topics in the future to clarify why it's being referenced. The idea is to keep it flexible, try not to make notes too specific or too generic, state the concept clearly so that searching and linking is less annoying as you add more.

# Cow Farming

It's important to know how [[cow stomach]]s process what you feed them so you can keep [[cow]]s healthy.
I found this interesting because XYZ...
Note that you can make the entities plural like "Cows.md" and make everything lower case, it's only a stylistic preference.

Here is a more fleshed out example with some more opinionated linking that demonstrates a decent breadcrumb trail between seemingly unrelated topics. I am not saying one or the other is bad, but in the case of searching, I find the isolation to be superior.

Another point that may be of interest to some. I would caution against giving personal data to AI, but when referencing notes, compact and easily digestible notes on specific topics, connected by clear breadcrumbs reduces the amount of irrelevant context and overhead needed to accomplish a task. Imagine loading several thousand words about a cow when you only need a few hundred about cow farming.

The same goes for the human brain when searching manually, the amount of information you have to wade through matters!

Obsidian’s quick switcher (Ctrl + o) is one of my favorite core plugins in Obsidian because it’s so dang responsive and doubles as a lightning-fast filename search. Obsidian is smart enough to ignore how files are named in searches, with or without special characters and a bunch of annoying regex nonsense. The way you name files primarily impacts the way linking between documents appears, so I use spaces in file names, even though it’s not required because it appears visually better as a preference.

  • Search: "Cow Farm"
  • Filename: Cow Farming.md
  • Filename: Cow_Farming.md
  • Filename: CowFarming.md

If you don’t use complex YAML frontmatter and metadata for your notes, you can stop here.

There is a strong case to be made for simplicity and not sorting all your notes into folders or harsh categories. I really liked a blog post by the creator of Obsidian which aligns with simplicity and outlines leaning on linking and meaning over classification, taxonomy, or folders.

But if you enjoy lots of fancy metadata, try not to wrap a new note in tags or categories until after you write down and think through it for a while. Maybe run it through Grammarly or at least spell check to catch anything insane before it propagates to several notes causing painful renaming and context issues.

It’s easy to force links and tags between notes because the graph view with all the pretty dots is amusing when there are tons of connections. The main focus for linking should be to create a simple breadcrumb trail in your notes so you can find it later, not to make it pretty. If you have too many links, it loses its meaning.

Then stop. Try to keep working on whatever you are working on and avoid the trap.

Once you are sure the topic is understood and can only be one of a handful of categories, then I would consider adding metadata classifications like “science” or whatever, but try keep these broad. I cannot tell you how many times in the beginning I had to come back and revise my tags because I eventually hit an edge case that causes it to no longer make sense. Even my example below could eventually hit an edge case, at what point does learning about cows become agriculture or remain biology?

---
category: [[agriculture]]
tags:
  - farming
  - ...
---
# Cow Farming

It's important to know how a [[cow stomach]] processes what you feed the cow so you can keep them healthy while [[farming]].
I found this interesting because XYZ...

---
category: [[biology]]
tags:
  - digestion
  - ...
---
# Cow Stomachs

[[Cow]] stomachs are divided into 4 compartments which aid in the digestion of grass more slowly in several stages.
(https://external-cow-source.zom)
I found this interesting because XYZ...

Maintaining Notes

The way notes are created and formatted directly correlates to how easily they can be maintained and found later on. This can turn into a huge time sink, especially as a perfectionist. In which case I highly recommend never using Obsidian in the first place, you will lose more time than you save.

Portability

Many note-taking apps, Obsidian in particular, have lots of customization options. This is one of Obsidian’s greatest strengths, you can adapt it to just about any format you want with the right plugins and themes, but it’s easy to abuse.

If done right, you can query everything in Obsidian down to an excruciating level of detail. But if done wrong, it feels like additional work, and the notes never feel quite right. Think of it like a spectrum, the more you customize it, the more complex it can become, and the harder it will be to maintain. You also have to consider if someone stops maintaining a plugin you become dependent on, what then?

Obsidian has a solution for this, it keeps everything it does in files in the root directory of a vault, making it portable and allowing the source control from the previous section to track it. This not only applies to Obsidian’s settings, but also to your plugins and plugin settings. If you keep this location .obsidian directory with all or the desired JSON contents tracked in source control, or at least keep it in a common location, every time you transfer or clone your notes, it should maintain the same structure.

Keeping track of the JSON files of your choosing makes the workflow semi-portable. This does not just apply to Obsidian, most applications keep configuration in a specific location in a similar manner. This helps with maintainability because you don't have to reload a giant list of all the plugins and tweaks you did every time.

Source Control

A long time ago, I created a Git repository with my notes in it for better control over the process, even before I started using Obsidian. If you're not familiar with source control, it's a way to track changes and, more importantly, revert changes if something goes wrong.

Consistently committing changes with a little note of what I was working on, I find, makes it easier to maintain. Think of it like notes for the notes that you don’t have to look at until you really need it. I can easily see a history of what I did from another perspective. If I make notes and do a poor job at labeling them, I can look through my git history and typically find somewhere in the past few days or weeks where it is.

If you hate Git, that’s fine, but I would recommend some kind of layer of software that can give you a sense of the history of your changes, even if it’s within Obsidian itself, is a must after a few years. I tried to add my own custom madness that didn’t work well and had to revert a few times now.
